Start here

How to build a short-essay answer

The exam lasts 90 minutes. About half applies entrepreneurship concepts to Knittr, and half applies Organizational Behavior concepts to new team situations. Definitions matter, but the grader also needs to see why a concept fits the facts and how it helps resolve the challenge.

Use C-E-A-R:

  1. Concept: Name and briefly define the most useful course concept.
  2. Evidence: Point to a specific action, statement, or result in the case.
  3. Analysis: Explain what the evidence reveals and why it matters.
  4. Response and result: Recommend a concrete action and state what it should improve.

This situation shows [concept], which means [brief definition]. We can see it when [case evidence]. This matters because [analysis]. The team should [specific response], which would [expected result].

Choose the smallest number of concepts that fully answers the prompt. Spend more space applying them than defining them. End with an action someone could actually observe, not a vague instruction to “communicate better” or “do more research.”

How to use this version

  • Learn explains the concept without depending on the case.
  • Application connects the concept to Knittr or an OB mini-case.
  • Practice shows how to turn the reasoning into an exam response.
  • Mark individual topics studied. A session is complete when all its topics are checked.
  • Search for a term such as persona, viability, psychological safety, or DiSC.

Knittr case hub

The case in one page

Kayden, a Bentley freshman, believes women who sew, knit, and crochet spend heavily on supplies, so he assumes the category is a “goldmine.” He does not knit and says he does not care about crafts. He builds a persona named Beatrice from TikTok: a 28-year-old who watches K-dramas, meets friends at cafés, owns many cats, and supposedly wants cheaper supplies online.

Kayden moves among several versions of Knittr: an online craft-supply store, a social network for knitters, and an AI that recommends projects and creates shopping lists. He says he is not a coder but could build the AI quickly. He has not established which customer problem matters, which offer he is testing, or whether AI improves the customer's experience.

Because he does not know anyone like Beatrice, Kayden holds a “focus group” with his mother, Kelly, and sisters, Kailey and Kynlee. He pitches Knittr before asking about their experiences, treats sarcasm as excitement, asks a participant to be more positive, and accepts his mother's praise as proof. The participants do not discuss their own crafting behavior or respond to one another as target customers.

Kayden then buys supplies. His value proposition promises a “robot friend,” a central shopping site, project recommendations, a social community, lower prices, and less effort. He does not know how to knit or program, has little money, and has not described suppliers, fulfillment, price, costs, or a revenue model. He plans to write a business plan, seek investors, build a full website, and possibly obtain a warehouse.

Facts, claims, and missing evidence

What the case establishes What Kayden assumes What still needs evidence
Kayden does not knit or enjoy crafts. Craft spending means Knittr is a strong opportunity. Which specific customers experience which repeated problem?
Beatrice came from TikTok impressions. She wants cheaper online supplies and AI suggestions. Do research patterns support this segment, pain, and buying criterion?
His mother and sisters reacted to a sales pitch. Their reactions validate the market. What do representative target customers actually do?
Knittr includes shopping, social, and AI ideas. More features create more value. Which single benefit is important enough to change behavior or earn payment?
Kayden bought supplies and wants outside funding. A plan and investment should come before testing. What can he test manually with an affordable loss?
He lacks craft expertise, coding skill, capital, and a delivery system. He can figure execution out later. Can a team and partner network deliver reliably and profitably?

The venture-wide diagnosis

Knittr is an idea supported by assumptions, not yet a demonstrated opportunity. The central recommendation is to pause major commitments without abandoning the idea. Kayden should study real crafters, define a narrow problem and target, test the riskiest assumption with a small manual prototype, and use observed behavior to revise the value proposition and business model. Each entrepreneurship session below examines a different part of that sequence.

Session 1Joint

Entrepreneurial Thought and Action

Entrepreneurial Thought and Action helps someone choose how to act when the future is uncertain. The session connects prediction, creation, available means, affordable loss, and learning. For Knittr, the question is whether Kayden should make a large plan from guesses or take a small step that produces evidence.

Topic map

  1. Prediction or causal logic
  2. Creation logic and CreAction
  3. Means at hand
  4. Affordable loss
  5. Act, learn, adjust, and intelligent failure
Knittr case hub

Prediction or causal logic

General concept

Prediction logic starts with a chosen goal, forecasts the future, identifies the resources required, and builds a plan to reach the expected return. It is useful when demand, costs, technology, and the path to execution are reasonably knowable.

Important distinction

Prediction is not automatically wrong. Its value depends on how much reliable information exists. A detailed forecast cannot make an uncertain customer problem more certain.

Knittr application

Kayden discusses a business plan, investors, a complete website, inventory, and a warehouse before he has valid customer evidence. Those actions depend on forecasts about demand, price, adoption, and execution that the case does not support. A large prediction-led commitment would amplify his unsupported assumptions.

Exam move

Name the uncertainty before criticizing the plan: Kayden does not know whether a specific segment has the problem, wants his solution, trusts AI, or will pay enough to cover delivery.

Creation logic and CreAction

General concept

Creation or effectual logic is useful under uncertainty. The entrepreneur begins with available means, takes a small action, observes the result, brings in committed people or resources, and adjusts the goal. In the course, combining thought with affordable action is called CreAction.

Knittr application

Instead of waiting for investment, Kayden could offer several actual knitters a manually prepared project recommendation and compatible shopping list. That action is small enough to change, exposes the offer to real users, and produces information about whether matching projects and supplies solves a meaningful problem.

Exam move

Do not describe CreAction as random improvisation. The action should test a named uncertainty and create evidence for the next decision.

Means at hand

General concept

Creation logic begins with three questions: Who am I? What do I know? Whom do I know? These reveal skills, interests, credibility, knowledge, and relationships the entrepreneur can use immediately.

Knittr application

Kayden's current means reveal important gaps: he does not knit, program, or have much money, and he does not know people like his imagined customer. His available strengths may include time, access to a college community, basic online tools, and the ability to recruit collaborators. He should bring an experienced crafter and potential users into the process rather than pretending the gaps do not matter.

Exam move

A means inventory is not only a list of strengths. Naming a gap helps explain which partner, experiment, or learning step should come next.

Affordable loss

General concept

Affordable loss limits a step by what the entrepreneur can accept losing if it fails. It asks, “What can I risk to learn?” rather than, “How large might the eventual return be?”

Knittr application

Buying a large inventory or signing for warehouse space would be difficult for Kayden to reverse. A few hours creating manual recommendations, a small prototype, or a limited preorder test would put far less money at risk while testing the same underlying assumptions.

Exam move

Specify the resource being limited—money, time, reputation, inventory, or commitment—and explain what the smaller loss buys in learning.

Act, learn, adjust, and intelligent failure

General concept

An intelligent failure is an unwanted result from a thoughtful, limited test of genuine uncertainty. It becomes useful when the team records what happened and changes the next action. Repeating a known error or ignoring available information is a mistake, not intelligent failure.

Knittr application

If target users reject a manual AI-style recommendation, the test could still be productive because it shows that the assumed benefit or delivery method needs revision. Buying inventory before running that test would not create the same disciplined learning.

Exam move

Kayden is relying on prediction logic while the customer problem and demand are uncertain. His plans for investors, a full site, and a warehouse show that he wants to commit major resources before learning whether knitters value the offer. He should use CreAction by manually creating project recommendations and supply lists for several actual knitters within a fixed time-and-money limit. Their behavior would help him adjust the offer before making a larger commitment.

Session 9Joint

Rocket Pitch

A Rocket Pitch makes the logic of an opportunity easy to follow. Its parts form one argument: a specific customer has an evidenced problem; the proposed solution creates a benefit; the offer differs from alternatives; the team gives a reason to believe; and the pitch ends with a clear next step.

Topic map

  1. Customer, problem, and solution chain
  2. Benefit rather than features
  3. Overt benefit, dramatic difference, and reason to believe
  4. Next step and request
  5. Team delivery and feedback
Knittr case hub

Customer, problem, and solution chain

General concept

A pitch should name the specific customer before describing the product. It should show the problem through evidence or a concrete behavior, then introduce the solution as a response to that problem.

Knittr application

Kayden currently begins with “all the ladies” who craft and moves quickly to an online store, social network, and AI recommender. Because neither the customer nor problem is focused, the audience cannot tell which solution is necessary. He should select a research-supported first target and show the exact point where that customer's current project-selection or supply-buying process breaks down.

Exam move

If removing the feature list leaves no clear problem, the pitch has not established its foundation.

Benefit rather than features

General concept

A feature describes what the product contains. A benefit describes the progress or improved result the customer receives. Asking “Which means that…?” helps translate a feature into a benefit.

Knittr application

AI recommendations, message boards, patterns, and a store are features. A possible benefit is beginning a suitable project faster with confidence that the pattern, skill level, yarn, and supplies match. Research must still show that customers value this result.

Exam move

Connect one feature to one valued result instead of claiming that a long list of features automatically creates value.

Overt benefit, dramatic difference, and reason to believe

General concept

A strong pitch answers three questions. Overt benefit: Why should the customer care? Dramatic difference: Why choose this over current alternatives? Real reason to believe: Why trust the promise?

Knittr application

Kayden claims convenience, community, lower prices, and personalized ideas, but he gives no evidence that one matters most or that Knittr can deliver it better than pattern sites, yarn shops, Ravelry, Etsy, blogs, social media, or friends. His lack of craft and technical expertise also weakens the reason to believe.

Exam move

The difference must matter to the customer, and the proof must support the specific promise. “It uses AI” does neither by itself.

Next step and request

General concept

A pitch should end with the next action the team wants from the audience. Early ventures often need access, expertise, participants, or a test commitment before they need large investment.

Knittr application

Kayden's first request should not be funding for a warehouse. A more defensible request would be introductions to target crafters, a craft expert who can help validate recommendations, or participants for a small prototype test.

Exam move

Match the request to the venture's current evidence. Investment is premature when the central customer and problem assumptions remain untested.

Team delivery and feedback

General concept

A team pitch should divide responsibility by idea, use clear handoffs, rehearse for time, and remove details that do not support the main claim. Feedback should improve the argument, not only the speaking style.

Mini-practice

If listeners remember the AI but cannot name the customer problem, the team should rewrite the logic before polishing slides or delivery.

Exam move

Knittr's pitch lacks a focused value proposition because it lists AI, shopping, and social features without establishing a specific customer or verified pain. Kayden should open with a researched segment, show evidence of one repeated problem, present one benefit, compare it with the customer's current method, and give proof that the team can deliver. He should end by asking for access to target users or expertise for the next test rather than immediately asking investors to fund a warehouse.

Session 3Entrepreneurship

Problem Solving and Idea Generation

This session explains how a venture moves from an interesting idea toward a credible opportunity. It begins with the customer's problem, expands the range of possible solutions, and delays evaluation until the team has generated real variety.

Topic map

  1. Idea versus opportunity
  2. Opportunity quality
  3. Customer problem and problem statement
  4. Divergent and convergent thinking
  5. Brainstorming process and rules
  6. AI in idea generation
Knittr case hub

Idea versus opportunity

General concept

An idea is an early insight about a problem or possible solution. An opportunity connects a defined customer and meaningful unmet need with a response that customers value, the team can deliver, and the model can sustain.

Knittr application

Knittr is currently an idea. Kayden knows that people spend money in a broad category, but category spending does not prove that his chosen customer has the problem he assumes, wants AI recommendations, or will switch from current alternatives.

Exam move

State what evidence would move the idea forward: repeated customer pain, unsatisfactory workarounds, prototype use, willingness to switch or pay, and a credible delivery path.

Opportunity quality

General concept

A promising opportunity is attractive because customers care enough to act, durable because the need and advantage can persist, timely because conditions support action now, and value-creating because it produces a result the customer values.

Knittr application

Kayden has not tested any of the four qualities. His broad observation that crafters spend money does not show attraction to Knittr, the durability of an AI advantage, the timing of the market, or customer value beyond his own feature ideas.

Exam move

Evaluate each quality separately. A venture can be timely because AI is popular while still failing to create a meaningful customer benefit.

Customer problem and problem statement

General concept

A customer problem describes the progress someone is trying to make, the point where the current process breaks down, and the important obstacle or tradeoff. It should not hide a preferred solution inside the problem.

As a [specific person in a context], I need a way to [make progress] without [important obstacle or tradeoff].

Knittr application

A testable starting statement is: “As a newer knitter with limited free time, I need a reliable way to choose a project that matches my skill and obtain compatible supplies without comparing scattered patterns, requirements, and stores.” The wording is a hypothesis, not a fact, until real customer evidence supports it.

Exam move

Do not write “customers need an AI app.” That makes Kayden's solution sound like the problem and prevents the team from considering alternatives.

Divergent and convergent thinking

General concept

Divergent thinking expands the number and variety of possibilities. Convergent thinking applies criteria to narrow them. Teams lose options when they judge each idea while it is being generated.

Knittr application

Kayden fixates on AI, a store, and a social network before he has framed the problem. The team should first generate many ways to help the chosen customer—including services, tools, partnerships, education, and changes to the buying process—then evaluate them using evidence.

Exam move

Show the sequence: define the challenge, diverge without judgment, cluster and combine, then converge using customer value and testability.

Brainstorming process and rules

General concept

Begin with a customer-centered “How might we?” question. Give participants quiet individual time before sharing. Pursue quantity, stay on topic, be visual, build on ideas, keep one conversation at a time, defer judgment, and welcome unusual ideas. Cluster options and evaluate only after the group has diverged.

Knittr application

Kayden could ask, “How might we help newer knitters move from wanting a project to starting one with confidence?” Actual crafters and people with different expertise should participate. Silent ideation would keep Kayden from anchoring everyone on AI.

Exam move

Explain why each step matters. Deferred judgment increases variety; silent generation protects independent thought; diverse participants contribute different information; later criteria prevent creativity from becoming random selection.

AI in idea generation

General concept

AI can produce variations, combinations, prompts, and perspectives. It cannot establish customer truth, desirability, feasibility, or responsible use. People must provide context, verify output, and test ideas with customers.

Knittr application

AI might help the team imagine project-matching experiences or summarize possible pain points. It cannot prove that crafters want AI recommendations, that a suggestion is technically correct, or that customers will trust it with browsing data.

Exam move

Effective brainstorming would help Kayden escape fixation on his first solution. The team should frame a customer-centered “How might we?” question, generate ideas independently and together without criticism, combine options, and only then evaluate them. AI can expand the list, but actual crafters and experiments must judge value and accuracy. This process creates multiple testable hypotheses instead of treating Kayden's preferred technology as the opportunity.

Session 5Entrepreneurship

Empathy and Design Thinking

Design thinking is a human-centered, iterative process. The session connects empathy, observation, discovery interviews, problem definition, ideation, prototyping, testing, and revision. Knittr's main weakness is that Kayden designs from stereotypes and pitches instead of learning from customer behavior.

Topic map

  1. Design-thinking cycle
  2. Empathy versus sympathy
  3. Observation and context
  4. Discovery interviews
  5. Question quality and neutral probing
Knittr case hub

Design-thinking cycle

General concept

The cycle is empathize → define → ideate → prototype → test → iterate. Teams may move backward as new evidence changes their understanding. The cycle creates value from the customer's perspective rather than protecting the founder's first idea.

Knittr application

Kayden jumps from TikTok impressions to a persona and from a persona to several solutions. He needs to return to empathy and definition before committing to the AI store or social network.

Exam move

Name the missing stage and show how it affects later stages. A weak empathy foundation produces an unreliable problem statement, prototype, and value proposition.

Empathy versus sympathy

General concept

Empathy means understanding another person's context, feelings, behavior, and needs well enough to see the situation from that person's perspective. Sympathy is feeling concern for someone. Empathy research requires curiosity and evidence; it is not pity or immediate problem-solving.

Knittr application

Kayden says he does not like crafts and does not know what “knitting gals” like. Those facts do not prevent him from learning, but his dismissive language and stereotypes show that he has not yet tried to understand customers on their terms.

Exam move

Recommend contact with real people in context, not simply a more detailed imaginary persona.

Observation and context

General concept

Observation reveals what people actually do, including forgotten steps, workarounds, environmental constraints, and contradictions between stated preferences and behavior.

Knittr application

Kayden could observe how knitters discover patterns, check skill level, calculate yarn needs, compare materials, ask for help, change plans when an item is unavailable, and decide whether to abandon a project.

Exam move

An observation should target a decision or process. “Watch knitters” is vague; “watch how they match a pattern to yarn and what happens when the recommended yarn is unavailable” can produce actionable evidence.

Discovery interviews

General concept

Discovery interviews explore real experiences before selling a solution. Recruit people beyond close friends and family, begin broadly, ask about recent past behavior, listen more than you speak, and probe neutrally for specific actions, feelings, constraints, and workarounds.

Knittr application

Kayden's family meeting begins with a detailed pitch. As a result, participants react to his claims instead of describing their own process. He should interview actual members of a defined target segment before mentioning Knittr or AI.

Exam move

Useful opening: “Tell me about the last knitting or crochet project you chose. Walk me through everything from the first idea to having the supplies ready.”

Question quality and neutral probing

General concept

Strong discovery questions are open, clear, neutral, and grounded in specific past behavior. Avoid leading questions, vague questions, two questions joined together, and predictions about what someone might do. Neutral probes include “What happened next?”, “Can you give an example?”, and “What made that difficult?”

Knittr application

“Would you use an AI crafting app?” introduces Kayden's solution, encourages politeness, and asks for an unreliable future prediction. A story about the participant's last project reveals actual behavior and current alternatives.

Exam move

Kayden lacks empathy evidence because he has not observed actual crafters and his family discussion pitches the solution before exploring experience. He should recruit target customers, ask about the last time they selected a project and bought supplies, and use neutral follow-ups about frustrations and workarounds. Those stories would help him define the real problem and may show that AI or low price is less important than he assumes.

Session 7Entrepreneurship

Value Proposition and Target Market

This session connects the people a venture serves with the reason they would choose its offer. A strong target is narrow enough to study and reach. A strong value proposition turns verified customer priorities into a clear benefit, meaningful difference, and credible promise.

Topic map

  1. Customer, consumer, payer, and decision-maker
  2. Segmentation and first target market
  3. Research-based persona
  4. Feature versus benefit
  5. Value proposition
  6. Overt benefit, dramatic difference, and proof
  7. Business model
Knittr case hub

Customer, consumer, payer, and decision-maker

General concept

The customer is willing and able to pay. The consumer or user experiences the product. The decision-maker chooses whether to buy, and a different person may influence the choice. A venture must know whose problem it solves and whose criteria control the purchase.

Knittr application

For an adult hobbyist, the payer, decision-maker, and user may be the same person. For a gift, class, or younger crafter, they may differ. Kayden's phrase “all the ladies” does not specify any role, context, or buying decision.

Exam move

If the roles differ, write a separate benefit and concern for each. A user may value a good project match while a payer cares more about price, waste, or confidence in product quality.

Segmentation and first target market

General concept

Segmentation divides a broad market into groups with shared demographics, geography, attitudes, behaviors, or needs. The first target market is the segment the venture chooses to understand, reach, and serve before expanding.

Knittr application

People who sew, crochet, and knit are not one useful segment. A testable starting target might be beginner and intermediate knitters who find patterns online, complete several projects per year, and struggle to match those patterns with suitable yarn. Research must confirm that the struggle is repeated and important.

Exam move

“Women” is a demographic descriptor, not a complete target. Add behavior, context, need, and reachable channel.

Research-based persona

General concept

A persona is a fictional representation built from repeated patterns in customer evidence. It should capture goals, pains, behavior, constraints, workarounds, buying criteria, and channels. A detail belongs only when it helps predict a relevant decision.

Knittr application

Beatrice's age, cats, K-dramas, and café meetings come from Kayden's TikTok impressions. The case gives no evidence that those traits affect project choice or supply purchases. Her assumed interest in lower prices is also unverified.

Exam move

Kayden should interview and observe people in a narrow segment, code repeated themes, and rebuild the persona from evidence. A valid persona summarizes research; it does not replace research.

Feature versus benefit

General concept

A feature is something the offer has or does. A benefit is the improved result the customer receives. Repeatedly add “which means that…” until the statement reaches an outcome the customer values.

Knittr application

“AI recommendations” is a feature. A possible benefit is reducing the time and uncertainty involved in finding a project that fits the user's skill and available materials. “Message board” is a feature; a possible benefit is receiving timely help from credible peers. Both benefits still require evidence.

Exam move

Never assume a technology is its own benefit. Connect it to a customer result and then ask what behavior would show that the result matters.

Value proposition

General concept

A value proposition explains why a specific customer should choose an offer over current alternatives. It should resonate with an important need, differentiate in a way the customer values, and substantiate the promise with a reason to believe.

For [target customer], who need [important progress or pain relief], [offer] is a [category] that [main benefit], because [credible reason to believe].

Knittr application

Kayden's “robot friend” statement combines shopping, project discovery, social connection, lower prices, and personalization. It does not identify a researched target, choose one central benefit, compare alternatives, or explain why Knittr can deliver accurately.

Exam move

A provisional rewrite is: “For newer knitters who lose time matching online patterns with suitable yarn, Knittr is a guided project-planning service that turns one chosen pattern into a compatible supply list, supported by recommendations checked with experienced crafters.” Every part should then be tested.

Overt benefit, dramatic difference, and proof

General concept

The overt benefit gives the customer a clear reason to care. The dramatic difference explains why this offer beats the current method. The real reason to believe supports the promise through evidence, expertise, process, partnerships, or demonstrated performance.

Knittr application

Saving effort could be an overt benefit. Combining a pattern match with a verified supply list could differ from browsing separate sources. Expert-reviewed recommendations or successful prototype results could become a reason to believe. Kayden currently has none of that proof.

Exam move

Do not use “innovative” as evidence. Name what is different, why that difference matters, and what makes the claim credible.

Business model

General concept

A business model explains how a venture creates value, delivers value, and captures value as revenue. The Business Model Canvas contains customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, and cost structure.

Knittr application

Knittr has fragments of several models: retail margin on supplies, possible platform activity, and AI recommendations. Kayden has not chosen who pays, what they buy, how Knittr reaches them, which partners supply goods or expertise, or whether revenue covers technology, inventory, shipping, returns, and acquisition.

Exam move

Beatrice is not a valid persona because Kayden created her from stereotypes rather than repeated customer evidence. His value proposition is also feature-heavy and unsupported. He should choose a narrow behavioral segment, research its goals and buying process, and state one benefit that improves a verified pain. He can then test whether target customers prefer that benefit over current alternatives and use the results to complete the business model.

Session 8Entrepreneurship

Prototyping

Prototyping turns an uncertain idea into something customers can react to or use. The goal is to learn about a named assumption before the team spends heavily. The quality of a prototype comes from the decision it informs, not how finished it looks.

Topic map

  1. Prototype as a learning tool
  2. Riskiest assumption
  3. Fidelity and the smallest credible test
  4. Observable evidence and success criteria
  5. Feedback and iteration
  6. Minimum viable product
  7. Concierge and manual tests
Knittr case hub

Prototype as a learning tool

General concept

A prototype is an early representation created to answer a question. It can be a sketch, role-play, paper flow, clickable screen, sample service, landing page, or small transaction. It is not automatically an early version of the entire product.

Knittr application

Kayden does not need an AI system or full store to learn whether people value help matching a project with supplies. A paper workflow or manually created recommendation can expose the same customer assumption faster.

Exam move

State the question before describing the prototype. Without a question, the team may build something impressive that produces no useful evidence.

Riskiest assumption

General concept

The riskiest assumption is a belief whose failure would most threaten the venture and which remains uncertain. Early tests should target high-importance, low-evidence assumptions.

Knittr application

One central assumption is that a meaningful segment struggles enough with project-and-supply matching to change its current behavior. AI accuracy, trust, willingness to pay, and retail economics matter later, but a venture has little value if the underlying problem is weak.

Exam move

Do not choose the easiest assumption to test. Explain why failure of the selected assumption would change or stop the venture.

Fidelity and the smallest credible test

General concept

Fidelity is how closely a prototype resembles the finished experience. Use only enough detail to make the target behavior credible. Too little realism may produce an invalid result; too much makes change slow and expensive.

Knittr application

Kayden could let a participant choose among a small set of projects and receive a hand-built list checked by an experienced crafter. The service feels real enough to test usefulness without building recommendation software, supplier integrations, or a warehouse.

Exam move

Match fidelity to the question. A landing page can test which promise earns sign-ups, but it cannot test whether recommendations are accurate or useful after purchase.

Observable evidence and success criteria

General concept

Decide before testing what behavior would strengthen or weaken the assumption. Strong evidence includes completion, return use, switching, referral, time saved, a deposit, or payment. Compliments and hypothetical enthusiasm are weaker.

Knittr application

Kayden could track whether users complete the matching flow, use the list to obtain supplies, begin the project, return for another recommendation, and pay or place a refundable deposit. He should also record abandonment and continued use of current alternatives.

Exam move

Set a threshold and include disconfirming evidence. “People liked it” cannot guide the next investment.

Feedback and iteration

General concept

After a test, separate observations from interpretation, compare the result with the success criteria, revise the assumption or design, and run the next test. Iteration means evidence changes the product, not that the team repeatedly adds features.

Knittr application

If users value the supply list but ignore project suggestions, Knittr should narrow around compatibility rather than adding a social network. If expert review makes the service too slow, the team has learned about feasibility as well as desirability.

Exam move

Explain what a positive, negative, and mixed result would change. This shows that the prototype is part of a decision process.

Minimum viable product

General concept

An MVP is the smallest usable version that can test whether the venture creates value. “Minimum” does not mean careless; the experience must be credible enough for the customer to use and judge.

Knittr application

A functioning service that gives a small target group accurate project-and-supply matches could be an MVP. A static mockup may be a prototype, but it is not an MVP if no one can receive the promised value.

Exam move

Distinguish an MVP from the first thing the team can technically launch. Its scope follows the learning goal and core value proposition.

Concierge and manual tests

General concept

In a concierge test, the founder performs by hand what technology may later automate. This tests whether customers value the outcome and reveals the steps required to deliver it.

Knittr application

Kayden and a knowledgeable crafter could interview a participant, select a suitable project, check pattern and yarn compatibility, and send a shopping list. The time, questions, errors, and user response would inform both product design and feasibility.

Exam move

Kayden should not build the complete platform first. His riskiest assumption is that a defined group of knitters values help matching projects with supplies. He should run a concierge prototype in which he and a craft expert manually create recommendations for a few target users, then measure completion, actual use, return interest, and willingness to pay. This limits sunk cost and lets evidence shape the next version before software or inventory makes change expensive.

Opportunity Evaluation

An opportunity must work through three separate lenses: customers must want it, the team must be able to deliver it, and the model must sustain itself. Evaluating only one lens creates false confidence.

Topic map

  1. Desirability
  2. Feasibility
  3. Viability
  4. Competition, alternatives, and current workarounds
  5. Resources, capabilities, and risk
  6. Responsible opportunity evaluation
Knittr case hub

Desirability

General concept

Desirability asks whether real target customers care enough to act. Useful evidence includes repeated pain, costly workarounds, prototype use, return behavior, switching, referrals, deposits, and payment.

Knittr application

Kayden has praise from his mother after a sales pitch, not evidence from a defined target. He has not shown that people struggle with project selection, want AI, prefer a combined store, or will change their current method.

Exam move

Test desirability first when the customer problem itself is uncertain. A product that can be built is not an opportunity if customers do not value it.

Feasibility

General concept

Feasibility asks whether the team and its partners can create and deliver the promised experience reliably. Consider skills, technology, data, suppliers, time, operations, quality, privacy, fulfillment, and returns.

Knittr application

Kayden lacks knitting expertise, coding skill, capital, supplier relationships, and a delivery system. Accurate recommendations may require structured pattern and product data plus craft judgment. He should test the workflow manually and identify partners before claiming easy automation.

Exam move

Do not reduce feasibility to “Can someone code it?” Delivery includes every capability required to keep the customer promise.

Viability

General concept

Viability asks whether the venture can capture enough value to sustain itself. Analyze who pays, price, demand, purchase frequency, gross margin, customer-acquisition cost, technology, labor, inventory, shipping, returns, and overhead.

Knittr application

Kayden assumes lower prices will attract customers but gives no cost structure or revenue model. A low-price promise combined with inventory, shipping, returns, software, and customer acquisition could make every sale unprofitable.

Exam move

Growth does not repair negative unit economics. If each transaction loses money, more transactions can increase the loss.

Competition, alternatives, and current workarounds

General concept

Direct competitors sell a similar solution. Indirect alternatives solve the same job differently. The current workaround and doing nothing also compete for the customer's time and money.

Knittr application

Knittr competes with yarn stores, pattern sites, Ravelry, Etsy, blogs, social media, search, friends, existing recommendation tools, and the customer's own experience. Kayden must show why the proposed result is meaningfully better than this complete set, not simply claim a new niche.

Exam move

Ask what the customer would do if Knittr did not exist. That answer often reveals the most important alternative.

Resources, capabilities, and risk

General concept

Map what the team controls, what it can obtain, and where it depends on partners. Prioritize assumptions whose failure would cause the greatest harm or expense.

Knittr application

Craft expertise and access to target users are immediate gaps. Product data, AI accuracy, privacy, supplier reliability, and fulfillment become larger risks if the model expands. A knowledgeable partner and a small manual test can reduce uncertainty before major investment.

Exam move

Connect every gap to a response: learn, recruit, partner, license, narrow the promise, or run a limited test.

Responsible opportunity evaluation

General concept

Evaluate social and environmental consequences alongside financial outcomes. Consider honest marketing, accessibility, privacy, supplier labor, packaging, production, waste, returns, and end-of-life impact. Avoid claims the evidence cannot support.

Knittr application

Personalized recommendations may use browsing or project data, so Knittr needs clear consent and careful handling. A lower-price retail model could increase packaging, shipping, returns, or excess inventory. Recommendations should not exaggerate sustainability or guarantee compatibility without evidence.

Exam move

Knittr has not yet established desirability, feasibility, or viability. Kayden needs customer behavior showing that a defined segment values the service, a manual delivery test showing that accurate recommendations and fulfillment are possible, and price-and-cost experiments showing a sustainable margin. He should compare Knittr with stores, pattern sites, social platforms, friends, and doing nothing. Only evidence across all three lenses would turn the idea into a credible opportunity.

Market Research

Market research collects evidence for a decision. The method must match the question, the sample must represent the people involved in that decision, and the researcher must actively reduce bias.

  6. Focus groups

Knittr case hub

Primary and secondary research

General concept

Primary research is collected directly for the current decision. Secondary research was previously collected by someone else. Secondary sources can frame a market or reveal patterns; primary research tests the venture's specific customer and offer.

Knittr application

TikTok posts and category-spending claims are secondary inputs. Even if accurate, they cannot establish that a chosen segment experiences Kayden's assumed problem or prefers Knittr. Interviews, observations, prototype behavior, and transactions would provide primary evidence.

Exam move

Use secondary research to improve the questions you ask, not as a substitute for customer-specific primary evidence.

Qualitative and quantitative research

General concept

Qualitative research explores how, why, language, motives, and process. Quantitative research measures how many, how often, how much, or which option performs better. Explore first when the problem is unclear; measure later when the team has a specific hypothesis.

Knittr application

Kayden first needs qualitative interviews and observation to understand project and supply decisions. After finding a repeated theme, he can use a survey, landing-page comparison, conversion measure, or price test to estimate frequency and compare segments.

Exam move

A few deep interviews can reveal a theme but cannot prove market size. A large survey with a bad question can measure the wrong idea precisely.

Matching the method to the question

General concept

Use observation for behavior in context, interviews for individual stories, focus groups for interaction and language, surveys for structured measurement, and experiments for causal or behavioral comparisons.

Knittr application

To learn how someone chooses yarn, observe or interview. To see how knitters discuss trusted advice, use a focus group. To compare two benefits, use a landing-page experiment. To estimate willingness to pay, use a realistic price or preorder test rather than asking whether the idea sounds good.

Exam move

Name the decision first, then choose the method. “Conduct research” is not a complete recommendation.

Sampling and representativeness

General concept

A sample should include people who represent the decision being studied. Convenience samples are easy to reach but may differ from the target. Qualitative samples seek useful depth and variation; quantitative samples need enough coverage for the intended inference.

Knittr application

Kelly, Kailey, and Kynlee are available family members, but the case does not show that they belong to the target segment. Their relationship with Kayden also creates social pressure. He should recruit actual members of a clearly defined segment through stores, groups, classes, and relevant online communities.

Exam move

Sampling bias cannot be repaired only by asking better questions. Both participant selection and question design matter.

Question bias

General concept

A leading question signals the desired response. A double-barreled question asks about multiple things at once. A vague question lacks a clear time, behavior, or context. A hypothetical question asks someone to predict behavior they may never perform.

Knittr application

Kayden pitches before collecting evidence, interprets sarcasm as support, asks Kynlee to be positive, and welcomes his mother's praise. These actions create moderator and confirmation bias. Questions about the last real project would be stronger than reactions to his proposed AI platform.

Exam move

Rewrite a biased question in the answer. For example: “Walk me through the last time you chose a pattern and bought its supplies. Where did the process slow down or change?”

Focus groups

General concept

A focus group is a moderated discussion, often with five to eight members of a target segment. It uses interaction to explore language, experiences, reactions, and disagreement. Use a neutral moderator, a short guide of open questions, balanced participation, neutral probes, and an exit question.

Knittr application

Kayden's family meeting is a pitch followed by reactions, not a useful focus group. He should recruit target participants, delay showing the concept until they discuss current behavior, invite them to respond to one another, and avoid defending Knittr.

Exam move

Six stronger questions are:

  1. Tell us about the last knitting or crochet project you worked on and how you chose it.
  2. Walk us through everything from choosing the project to having the supplies ready.
  3. Where did that process become confusing, frustrating, expensive, or slow?
  4. How do you decide whether a pattern, yarn, price, and skill level are a good match?
  5. After trying this prototype, what felt useful, unhelpful, or uncomfortable?
  6. What have we missed that would matter before you changed your current method?

Analysis and disconfirming evidence

General concept

Separate what participants said or did from the team's interpretation. Look for repeated behavior, strong emotion, workarounds, contradictions, segment differences, and evidence that challenges the preferred idea. Turn themes into hypotheses for later tests.

Knittr application

Kayden treats praise as validation and sarcasm as excitement while rejecting criticism. A stronger analysis would record every response accurately, seek negative cases, compare themes across target participants, and state the limits of the sample.

Exam move

Confirmation bias appears not only in questions but also in what the researcher notices, records, and reports.

The 4Ps and revenue drivers

General concept

The 4Ps are product (offer and benefit), price (what is charged and why), place (channel and access), and promotion (how the target learns about it). Revenue depends on the number and quality of customers, purchase frequency, selling process or channel, and price.

Knittr application

Kayden has several product ideas and a vague lower-price claim, but no channel strategy, promotion evidence, demand estimate, purchase-frequency assumption, or tested price. Research should address each decision separately rather than treating broad craft spending as revenue proof.

Exam move

Kayden's family group does not validate Knittr because it uses a convenience sample and a biased moderator. He pitches first, discourages criticism, and treats praise as proof. He should recruit five to eight people from a defined target segment, ask open questions about recent behavior before showing the concept, invite discussion, and search for disagreement. The qualitative themes can define hypotheses, but later surveys, experiments, and price tests are needed to measure demand and viability.

Culture and Norms

Culture explains the shared assumptions and repeated behavior that tell members how work is really done. This session moves from broad culture to specific norms, artifacts, and team agreements.

Topic map

  1. Organizational culture
  2. Rules versus norms
  3. Visible and invisible culture
  4. Team charter and reinforcement

Organizational culture

General concept

Culture is the shared system of values, beliefs, assumptions, and norms that guides how members interpret situations and act. It appears in repeated behavior, stories, rituals, rewards, status, and what leaders tolerate.

Mini-case application

A team says it values preparation but regularly praises last-minute heroics. The repeated reward teaches members that crisis work, not preparation, earns status.

Exam move

Identify the repeated pattern and reinforcement. A single late meeting may be an event; a tolerated pattern becomes culture.

Rules versus norms

General concept

A rule is explicit and often written. A norm is an informal expectation formed through repeated behavior and social reinforcement. When the two conflict, members often follow what the group actually rewards.

Mini-case application

The charter says meetings begin at 6:00, but the group waits for two influential members who arrive at 6:15. Everyone learns that the real start time is 6:15.

Exam move

Recommend an observable response: start on time, record decisions for absent members, and apply the expectation consistently.

Visible and invisible culture

General concept

Visible artifacts include schedules, language, dress, workspace, rituals, and policies. Invisible culture includes assumptions about status, effort, conflict, risk, trust, and who is allowed to influence decisions.

Mini-case application

An open seating plan may look inclusive while senior members still interrupt newcomers. The artifact and underlying assumption point in different directions.

Exam move

Do not infer a value from an artifact alone. Explain the behavior and assumption the artifact may or may not support.

Team charter and reinforcement

General concept

A team charter makes purpose, roles, decisions, meetings, communication, conflict, participation, and accountability explicit. Strong norms begin with “We will,” describe observable behavior, and change when experience reveals a gap.

Mini-case application

“We will respect each other” is too vague to guide a tense meeting. “We will hear each person's evidence before deciding and criticize ideas without labeling people” is observable.

Exam move

The team has created an informal norm that 6:15 is acceptable because it repeatedly waits for influential late members. That behavior overrides the written 6:00 rule and teaches everyone that status excuses lateness. The team should begin at 6:00, document decisions for anyone absent, and apply the rule consistently. Repeated reinforcement would turn the charter into the lived culture.

Emotional Intelligence

Emotional intelligence is the ability to notice emotions, understand their effects, and use that awareness to manage behavior and relationships. The four abilities build from recognizing oneself to working effectively with others.

Topic map

  1. Self-awareness
  2. Self-management
  3. Social awareness and relationship management
  4. Resonant leadership and trust

Self-awareness

General concept

Self-awareness means recognizing one's emotions, triggers, patterns, strengths, limits, and impact on others.

Mini-case application

A leader who notices “I am interpreting this incomplete draft as disrespect” can separate the feeling from the facts before acting.

Exam move

Name both the emotion or trigger and the interpretation it may distort.

Self-management

General concept

Self-management means regulating impulses, staying adaptable, and choosing a response that serves the goal. It does not require hiding emotion.

Mini-case application

Instead of sending an angry group message, the leader pauses, checks the assigned role and deadline, and asks the writer what happened.

Exam move

Show a specific pause-and-check behavior. “Calm down” is not a process.

Social awareness and relationship management

General concept

Social awareness notices other people's emotions, needs, context, and group dynamics. Relationship management uses that understanding to communicate, repair trust, handle conflict, and move people toward a shared goal.

Mini-case application

After recognizing that a public accusation embarrassed a teammate, the leader acknowledges the impact privately and publicly clarifies the process problem without blaming the person.

Exam move

Self-management prevents further damage; relationship management repairs the relationship and changes the interaction.

Resonant leadership and trust

General concept

Resonant leaders recognize emotional tone and create productive connection. Trust depends on authenticity (genuine and consistent behavior), logic (sound reasoning and competence), and empathy (understanding and care).

Mini-case application

A leader may care deeply but lose trust if the team's plan is confusing. Another may be technically correct but lose trust through dismissive delivery. Diagnose which part is weak.

Exam move

The leader shows weak self-awareness and self-management by treating frustration as proof that the writer does not care and reacting publicly before checking the facts. The unconfirmed role and deadline also reveal a process failure. The leader should acknowledge the premature accusation, ask what each person understood, and document the owner, deliverable, standard, and deadline. This repairs empathy and authenticity while improving the logic of the team's process.

Positive Teaming and Psychological Safety

This session explains what makes a team effective and why members must feel safe enough to speak up. It moves from the three outcomes of an effective team to the specific behaviors, conflicts, and leadership choices that produce them.

Topic map

  1. Team effectiveness
  2. Psychological safety
  3. Failure versus mistake
  4. Task conflict and relationship conflict
  5. Process gains, process losses, and leadership levers

Team effectiveness

General concept

An effective team produces three outcomes: performance (the output meets stakeholder needs), learning and adaptability (members and the team become more capable), and member well-being and growth (the work stays sustainable and worthwhile). Supporting conditions include the right mix of people, trust, clear roles and decisions, and alignment with the larger system.

Mini-case application

A team earns an A on its project, but two members wrote nearly everything, nobody learned the analysis, and three members say they never want to work together again. The grade shows performance, not effectiveness.

Exam move

Name which of the three outcomes is failing before recommending a fix. A performance problem and a well-being problem need different interventions.

Psychological safety

General concept

Psychological safety is a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Members can ask questions, admit uncertainty, disagree, suggest unusual ideas, and discuss errors without expecting humiliation or exclusion. It does not mean constant agreement, low standards, or freedom from accountability.

Mini-case application

A quiet member noticed a calculation error a week before the deadline but said nothing because the leader had mocked an earlier question. The team lost information it already had.

Exam move

Safety is built through specific behaviors: leaders admit their own fallibility, ask directly for dissent, respond to bad news with curiosity, thank people for raising errors, and follow up so speaking up produces visible value.

Failure versus mistake

General concept

A failure is an unwanted result from a thoughtful test of genuine uncertainty, and it produces useful information. A mistake ignores known information, violates an established process, or repeats a known error.

Mini-case application

A team pilots a new survey channel that draws few responses: that is a productive failure. A team forgets to proofread after the charter required a final check: that is a mistake.

Exam move

Classify the unwanted result first. Productive failures deserve learning reviews; mistakes deserve process fixes and accountability.

Task conflict and relationship conflict

General concept

Task conflict debates evidence, assumptions, and choices, and it can improve decisions. Relationship or status conflict attacks people and damages the team. Useful responses restate the shared goal, ask for evidence, distribute airtime, name emotional escalation, and agree on a decision process.

Mini-case application

A disagreement about which market to target turns into “You always ignore my ideas.” The team has moved from task conflict to relationship conflict.

Exam move

Show how to return the conflict to the task: separate the person from the idea, compare options against agreed criteria, and decide how the team will choose.

Process gains, process losses, and leadership levers

General concept

Teams gain from combined knowledge, motivation, error checking, and a shared map of expertise. They lose value through social loafing, poor coordination, dominant speakers, fear, hidden information, and premature consensus. Leaders intervene through design (purpose, membership, roles, resources), launch (shared goals and norms), and process management (monitoring and adjusting). A team may need a relaunch when membership, purpose, or assumptions change.

Mini-case application

A new member joins halfway through the semester, roles were never reassigned, and the same two people still speak first in every meeting.

Exam move

The team is losing performance and learning through process losses: unclear roles after a membership change and dominant speakers who prevent quieter members from contributing. Members also lack psychological safety, since earlier questions were dismissed. The team should relaunch by reassigning roles, inviting each member to share concerns before discussion, and thanking anyone who raises a problem. This would surface hidden information and spread the work more fairly.

Diversity of Thought and Practice — DiSC

This session gives teams a shared language for behavioral and communication preferences. It moves from the four DiSC styles to flexing communication and designing team processes that use different strengths.

Topic map

  1. The four DiSC styles
  2. Flexing communication
  3. Diversity of thought and avoiding stereotypes

The four DiSC styles

General concept

DiSC describes preferences, not intelligence, worth, or fixed ability. Everyone can use all four styles.

Style Common strengths Possible challenge Helpful communication
D — Dominance/Driver Direct, fast, decisive, results-focused May be impatient or overlook input Lead with the result, options, and next action
I — Influencing/Expressive Energetic, persuasive, social, idea-generating May skip details or commit too quickly Invite ideas, then confirm deadlines and details in writing
S — Steady/Supporter Calm, patient, dependable, cooperative May avoid conflict or need time with change Explain changes, be supportive, and allow time to respond
C — Conscientious/Analytical Accurate, logical, careful, quality-focused May overanalyze or become stuck in detail Bring evidence, structure, expectations, and thinking time

Mini-case application

One member wants to submit the pitch tonight and move on. Another wants two more days to check every number. The first shows D preferences; the second shows C preferences.

Exam move

Describe the behavior you observe and the preference it suggests. Do not label the person.

Flexing communication

General concept

Flexing means adapting your communication so the team works better. A D can pause and invite input. An I can document commitments. An S can raise disagreement earlier. A C can name the minimum evidence needed for a timely decision.

Mini-case application

For the D-versus-C conflict above, the team sets a decision deadline and agrees on the minimum evidence needed for a reversible test. Both preferences are respected and neither is declared correct.

Exam move

Write the same message two ways. For a D: “Here is the result, two options, and the decision we need by Friday.” For an S: “Here is what is changing, why, and how you can give input before Friday.”

Diversity of thought and avoiding stereotypes

General concept

Different expertise, networks, backgrounds, and personalities can increase creativity and reduce groupthink, but only when team norms let that information surface and influence the work. A profile is a prompt for conversation, not permission to predict everything about a teammate.

Mini-case application

A team assigns all spreadsheet work to its C member and all presenting to its I member. The work gets done, but nobody learns, and the C member's ideas about the pitch are never heard.

Exam move

The team is using DiSC as a label instead of a shared language. Assigning work only by profile limits learning and keeps the C member's pitch ideas out of the discussion. The team should ask each member how they prefer to contribute, rotate at least one role, and agree that the I member confirms details in writing while the C member reviews the pitch logic. This would use both strengths and widen everyone's skills.

Inclusion and Identity

This session explains how identity shapes belonging and influence in a team. It moves from personal and social identity to the separate questions of diversity, inclusion, and belonging, and then to the practices that make inclusion real.

Topic map

  1. Personal and social identity
  2. Diversity, inclusion, and belonging
  3. Bias, faultlines, and subgroups
  4. Inclusive leadership and decisions

Personal and social identity

General concept

Personal identity includes the characteristics and experiences that shape how a person understands themselves. Social identity connects a person with groups that may influence belonging, status, opportunity, and how others respond. Some identities are visible; others are invisible or depend on context. An identity map builds awareness; it must not force disclosure or reduce a person to a category.

Mini-case application

A team assumes its only international student will handle all global-market research. The assumption turns one visible identity into an assignment the student never chose.

Exam move

Do not assume which identity matters to someone or how they experience it. Ask about interests and expertise instead.

Diversity, inclusion, and belonging

General concept

  • Diversity: Who is present, and what differences exist?
  • Inclusion: Whose voice, information, and perspective can influence the work?
  • Belonging: Do members feel accepted as legitimate parts of the group?

A team can be diverse without being inclusive.

Mini-case application

The leader asks a new member for ideas in every meeting, then never uses any of them. That gives the appearance of participation without real influence.

Exam move

Treat the three terms as separate questions and answer each with evidence from the case.

Bias, faultlines, and subgroups

General concept

Bias can affect whose ideas get credit, who is interrupted, and who receives high-visibility work. Faultlines divide a team into stable subgroups, such as veteran and new members, so information stays inside each group. Cross-subgroup tasks, shared goals, transparent decisions, and a wider team identity reduce the division.

Mini-case application

Three returning members meet before every official meeting and arrive with decisions already made. The two new members learn about choices after they are final.

Exam move

Look for patterns in outcomes, not just intentions: who speaks, who is credited, and who learns about decisions first.

Inclusive leadership and decisions

General concept

Inclusive leaders distribute airtime, prevent repeated interruption, ask for views before senior members anchor the discussion, use clear decision criteria, explain how input affected the final choice, and rotate meaningful responsibilities.

Mini-case application

The leader collects written ideas before discussion, pairs new and returning members on tasks, and summarizes how each person's input changed the plan.

Exam move

The team is diverse but not inclusive, because a faultline between returning and new members keeps decisions inside the veteran subgroup. The new members are asked for ideas, but their input does not influence choices made in advance. The leader should move all decisions into the full meeting, collect written ideas before discussion, and pair members across subgroups. This would give new members real influence and restore shared ownership of the work.
