Prepared from the FME study guide, class slides, assigned readings, activities, and Sean's discovery-interview work.

## 1. What the exam is actually testing

The exam lasts 90 minutes. About half covers entrepreneurship and half covers organizational behavior (OB) and team leadership. Every question is a short essay. The entrepreneurship questions apply concepts to the Knittr case. The OB questions apply concepts to new mini-cases about common FME team situations.

Definitions alone will not earn a strong answer. The study guide says the key is to explain how a concept fits the facts of a case and how it can resolve the challenge.

### The answer strategy: C-E-A-R

Use this four-part memory aid, built directly from the study guide's directions, for almost every response:

1. **Concept:** Name and briefly define the best course concept.
2. **Evidence:** Point to a specific fact, action, or quotation from the case.
3. **Analysis:** Explain why that evidence reveals the problem. This is the reasoning step.
4. **Response and result:** Recommend a concrete action and state what it should improve.

You can write this as one tight paragraph or repeat it for several concepts. A useful sentence pattern is:

> This situation shows **[concept]**, which means **[brief definition]**. We can see it when **[specific case evidence]**. This matters because **[cause/effect analysis]**. The team should **[specific action]**, which would **[expected result]**.

Weak answer: “The team needs psychological safety. Psychological safety is important.”

Strong answer: “The team lacks psychological safety, meaning members do not believe it is safe to take interpersonal risks. When Maya's suggestion is laughed at and she stops contributing, the team loses information and begins moving toward premature consensus. The facilitator should acknowledge the dismissive response, invite Maya to explain her idea, and establish a norm that questions come before criticism. That response makes future dissent safer and improves the team's ability to learn.”

### Fast exam workflow

- Circle the action words in the prompt: explain, compare, recommend, diagnose, or apply.
- Mark two or three concrete facts in the case before writing.
- Choose the smallest number of concepts that fully explains the problem.
- Use the concept's exact course language, but spend more space on application than definition.
- End with observable actions, not “communicate better” or “do more research.”
- If asked for several items, number them so the grader can see that every part is answered.

- **Session 2: Culture and Norms**
  - **Culture:** Shared values, assumptions, and repeated behavior that show how work is really done.
  - **Rules and norms:** Written expectations compete with the informal behavior the group actually rewards.
  - **Team charter:** Make roles, decisions, meetings, conflict, and accountability observable.

  - **Four abilities:** Self-awareness → self-management → social awareness → relationship management.

  - **D:** Direct, fast, results-focused.

  - **Identity:** Personal and social, visible and invisible, and shaped by context.
  - **Diversity:** Who is present? **Inclusion:** Who has influence? **Belonging:** Who feels accepted?
  - **Leadership:** Surface bias, prevent subgroup faultlines, distribute airtime, rotate meaningful roles, and explain decisions.

## 2. Knittr: the case in one page

Kayden believes women who knit and crochet spend heavily on supplies, so he assumes a profitable opportunity exists. He creates “Beatrice,” a 28-year-old knitter with cats who watches K-dramas and meets friends at cafés, based mainly on TikTok. He assumes she wants cheaper online supplies. He then proposes several different offers: an online supply store, a social network, and AI-generated project suggestions with shopping lists.

His research is badly biased. He does not know people in the target market, so he questions his mother and sisters in a family “focus group.” He opens by pitching the venture, asks for reactions, treats sarcasm as support, discourages negative feedback, and accepts his mother's praise as confirmation. The participants do not appear to be selected members of the proposed target market, and he never gets them to discuss experiences with one another.

Kayden buys supplies before testing the problem or solution. He does not knit, code, or have much money, yet he plans to seek investors, build a full website, and possibly obtain a warehouse. His value proposition is vague, feature-heavy, stereotyped, and unsupported. He has not shown that crafters want AI recommendations, that they cannot already find project ideas, that low price is their main concern, or that he can deliver the product profitably.

### The biggest case problems

| Course idea | Evidence in Knittr | What the evidence means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idea versus opportunity | Kayden jumps from hearing that crafters spend money to building Knittr. | Spending in a category does not prove that a specific customer has a painful unmet need or will switch. |
| Empathy and desirability | He dislikes knitting and has not observed or interviewed actual crafters. | He is designing from assumptions and stereotypes rather than customers' lived experiences. |
| Persona quality | “Beatrice” comes from TikTok impressions rather than research. | A persona should summarize verified patterns in real data, not decorate a guess. |
| Research bias | He pitches first, questions family, rejects criticism, and welcomes praise. | Leading questions, convenience sampling, social pressure, and confirmation bias make the evidence unreliable. |
| Value proposition | The statement emphasizes a “robot friend,” AI, and many products. | It lists features without proving a focused benefit, target customer, dramatic difference, or reason to believe. |
| Feasibility | He lacks knitting knowledge, coding skill, capital, suppliers, and a delivery plan. | The team cannot yet demonstrate that it can create and deliver the promised experience. |
| Viability | No price, margins, acquisition plan, demand estimate, or cost structure is tested. | He has not shown a repeatable business model that captures value as revenue. |
| CreAction | He wants investors, a finished website, and a warehouse before small tests. | He is using big planning and committing resources too early rather than limiting acceptable loss. |
| AI judgment | He assumes AI is the source of value. | AI can generate options, but customers and experiments must judge usefulness, trust, and willingness to pay. |

### Best overall recommendation

Kayden should pause major spending without abandoning the idea. He should return to the problem, recruit actual knitters and crocheters, observe how they choose projects and buy supplies, and conduct open-ended interviews. He can then form a research-based target segment and problem statement. Next, he should test the riskiest assumptions with a low-cost prototype, such as a manual project recommendation and shopping list sent to a few users, before building AI or holding inventory. The results should determine the value proposition, business model, and next small experiment.

## 3. Entrepreneurship concepts

### Entrepreneurial Thought and Action: prediction and CreAction

Entrepreneurial Thought and Action (ET&A) combines two decision logics. The goal is to choose the logic that matches how knowable the situation is.

| Prediction or causal logic | CreAction or effectual logic |
| --- | --- |
| Best when the future is more knowable | Best when the future is uncertain |
| Large plan before action | Small actions that create new information |
| Expected return | Acceptable loss |
| Linear execution | Iterative steps that change with what you learn |
| Optimize an existing path | Experiment to find new paths |
| Wait until required resources are secured | Start with what you have at hand |
| Avoid failure at all costs | Embrace and leverage failure |
| Competitive orientation | Collaborative orientation |
| “To the extent we can predict the future, we can control it.” | “To the extent we can create the future, we don't need to predict it.” |

When facing the unknown, the slides recommend taking small steps with what is at hand, limiting the risk of each step, building on good and bad results, involving people or resources that can help, and acting on something you actually care about.

**Knittr application:** Seeking investors, planning a warehouse, and building a full platform are prediction-heavy moves based on weak evidence. A CreAction approach would use Kayden's current resources to run a manual recommendation test, cap spending at an acceptable amount, involve knowledgeable crafters, and revise the idea based on results.

### Idea versus opportunity

An idea is an initial insight about a problem, a poor existing solution, or a possible use of technology. Ideas are common and often product-focused. An opportunity is customer-focused and connects a defined customer and unmet need with a solution, realistic costs, potential upside, and room for growth.

A promising opportunity is:

- **Attractive:** customers care enough to act.
- **Durable:** the need and advantage will last long enough to build a venture.
- **Timely:** market conditions make this a sensible moment.
- **Value-creating:** the offer produces a meaningful benefit for the user or customer.

Customers buy benefits, not a collection of product features. Talking to customers, brainstorming, prototyping, and learning are what develop an idea into an opportunity.

### Desirability, feasibility, and viability

Human-centered design begins with desirability.

| Lens | Core question | Evidence to collect | Knittr's current gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Desirability | Do real people want or need this? | Observations, stories, current workarounds, repeated pain, prototype behavior, willingness to switch or pay | No valid customer evidence; AI and low price are assumptions |
| Feasibility | Can this team create and deliver it? | Skills, technology, suppliers, partners, time, operations, resources, privacy and quality tests | No knitting expertise, coding ability, supply system, or delivery capability |
| Viability | Can it sustain a business model? | Revenue streams, price, frequency, market size, acquisition, costs, margins, competition, and social/environmental effects | No tested revenue model, economics, or path to customers |

An opportunity must reach the intersection of all three. A desirable product that loses money is not viable. A profitable-sounding technology that the team cannot deliver is not feasible. A feasible, profitable product that customers do not value is not desirable.

### Design thinking and human-centered design

Design thinking is a human-centered, iterative, and team-based process for creating value. It is a practice, not a one-time checklist.

1. **Empathize:** Observe and interview people in the context of their lives.
2. **Define:** Turn what was learned into a customer-centered problem statement.
3. **Ideate:** Generate many possible solutions before selecting one.
4. **Prototype:** Make the idea concrete at low cost.
5. **Test and iterate:** Watch real users interact with it, learn, and revise.

The central shift is from “What product do I want to build?” to “What problem is the customer already experiencing?”

### Observation and empathy

Observation means seeing behavior in context with fresh eyes. Look for what people actually do, including workarounds and small details, instead of seeing only what you expected. Notice who experiences the problem, who does not, who cannot use the normal solution, and which businesses or people benefit from the status quo.

Empathy is not sympathy and is not immediately entering “fixing mode.” It means understanding another person's feelings, circumstances, behaviors, and needs well enough to see the problem from that person's perspective.

For Knittr, useful observation could occur in local yarn stores, crafting groups, online communities, or classes. Kayden should notice how people choose a project, compare yarn, seek help, abandon projects, share work, and respond when supplies are unavailable.

### Discovery interviews

Discovery interviews explore the problem before selling a solution.

- Interview people outside close friends and family.
- Prepare a guide but follow important new directions.
- Start broad, then become specific.
- Ask open “how,” “why,” and “tell me about the last time” questions.
- Ask for stories, feelings, and real past behavior rather than predictions.
- Listen more than you speak.
- Record careful notes and exact short quotations as evidence.
- Look for frustration, anger, disappointment, workarounds, and the current status quo.
- Remain willing to be wrong and reflect on your own biases.

A weak question is “Would you use an AI crafting app?” It leads the participant toward the founder's solution and invites an unreliable hypothetical answer. A stronger question is “Tell me about the last time you chose a new crafting project. What did you do from the first idea to buying the supplies?”

### Defining the problem

A useful problem statement is written from the customer's perspective, broad enough to support creativity, and narrow enough to guide action.

Template:

> As a **[specific person in a context]**, I need a way to **[make progress or solve a pain]** without **[important tradeoff or obstacle]**.

Possible Knittr version, which still requires research:

> As a newer knitter with limited free time, I need a reliable way to choose a project that matches my ability and buy compatible supplies without comparing scattered patterns, yarn requirements, and stores.

### Brainstorming

Brainstorming creates a large and varied solution space. It matters because teams otherwise fixate on the first familiar product, while multiple voices can combine ideas into options no one person would have produced.

The class rules are:

- Go for quantity.
- Stay focused on the topic.
- Be visual.
- Build on and combine others' ideas.
- Have one conversation at a time.
- Defer judgment.
- Encourage wild ideas.

A strong process separates idea generation from evaluation:

1. Define a customer problem or “How might we?” question.
2. Warm up with a low-stakes creativity exercise.
3. Give everyone quiet individual ideation time so early speakers do not anchor the group.
4. Post all ideas, group related problems, and agree on the problem area.
5. Generate solutions as a team while enforcing the rules and equal participation.
6. Use AI to add numerous or strange possibilities, while humans supply context and judgment.
7. Categorize ideas as grounded, blue-sky, or “spaced out.” Look for ways to move useful pieces toward a testable blue-sky concept.
8. Only after divergence, evaluate ideas through customer value, desirability, feasibility, viability, and a small test.

### Using AI as a brainstorming partner

AI works well for producing many variations, combining team interests, exploring trends, suggesting unusual options, and adopting different perspectives. It is weak at deciding which ideas are practical or truly valuable to a specific customer. It may invent supporting evidence, so facts must be verified.

Treat AI as a co-pilot. Useful follow-ups include testing an idea under a zero-dollar budget, an unlimited budget, or no feasibility constraints; comparing the idea with the team's capabilities; and asking for fieldwork that could build empathy. Do not let AI replace observation, interviews, or human judgment.

### Target market, customer, and consumer

A customer is willing and able to pay. A consumer uses the product. They may be different people, so a venture must identify who decides, who pays, and who uses.

**Market segmentation** divides a broad market into groups with common needs, traits, interests, or behavior. The **target market** is the well-defined group the venture chooses to serve first.

Segments may be defined by:

- Demographics: age, income, occupation, or other measurable traits.
- Geography: location or neighborhood.
- Psychographics: lifestyle, priorities, identity, or interests.
- Behavior and need: frequency, current workaround, occasion, or intensity of pain.

“Women who craft” is far too broad. A possible starting segment is “beginner and intermediate knitters in the United States who complete several projects per year, choose patterns online, and struggle to match patterns with suitable yarn.” Research must show whether this group is attractive.

### Customer personas

A persona is a fictional representation of an ideal customer that is grounded in real interviews, polls, observations, demographics, and behavior. It gives the team a “north star” for product, marketing, operations, and channel decisions.

A useful persona explains:

- How the problem appears in daily life.
- Current behavior and workarounds.
- Needs, pains, goals, and decision criteria.
- How the person discovers, buys, and uses a solution.
- Relevant activities, brands, constraints, and future goals.

“Beatrice” is weak because her cats and K-dramas are unverified details, while the claimed purchasing pain is only Kayden's assumption. Persona detail is valuable only when it helps predict a decision.

### Value proposition

A value proposition explains why a target customer chooses one offer over alternatives. It should:

- **Resonate:** solve an important need or pain.
- **Differentiate:** offer a meaningful advantage over direct and indirect alternatives.
- **Substantiate:** give a real reason to believe the promise can be delivered.

Features are product facts; benefits are what the customer gains. Convert a feature to a benefit by adding “which means that…” until the sentence reaches a result the customer values.

Course template:

> For **[specific target audience]**, who need or want **[pain solved or desire fulfilled]**, **[product]** is a **[category]** that **[compelling benefit]**, because **[evidence or reason to believe]**.

Research-dependent Knittr draft:

> For beginner and intermediate knitters who lose time checking whether a pattern, yarn, and skill level fit together, Knittr is a guided project-planning service that turns one project choice into a compatible supply list, because each recommendation is checked against pattern requirements and crafter feedback.

This is stronger than Kayden's statement because it identifies one segment, one pain, one benefit, and a testable reason to believe. It intentionally does not promise AI until research shows that AI matters to customers.

For a pitch, also remember:

- **Overt Benefit:** Why should the customer care?
- **Real Reason to Believe:** Why should the customer believe the promise?
- **Dramatic Difference:** Why choose this over alternatives?

### Business model and Business Model Canvas

A business model describes how a company creates value, who receives it, how the company delivers it, and how the company captures value as revenue. The nine canvas blocks are:

1. Customer segments
2. Value proposition
3. Channels
4. Customer relationships
5. Revenue streams
6. Key resources
7. Key activities
8. Key partners
9. Cost structure

A good product can fail inside a bad business model. Webvan offered fast grocery delivery but paired it with capital-intensive warehouses and price-sensitive customers.

For Knittr, Kayden must decide whether he is a retailer, marketplace, subscription recommendation service, affiliate platform, social network, or some testable combination. Each model changes inventory, partners, revenue, costs, and risks.

### Market research

Market research is the systematic design, collection, analysis, and reporting of data relevant to a business decision. Entrepreneurs use it to validate a target market, shape the 4Ps, identify revenue drivers, clarify competitive advantage, and understand the business model.

| | Primary: collected by the team for this purpose | Secondary: already collected by others |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qualitative: descriptive and exploratory | Interviews, focus groups, observation | Company materials, industry narratives, archived accounts |
| Quantitative: numerical and confirmatory | Surveys, experiments, transaction tests | Financial reports, market reports, published surveys |

Start with qualitative research when the team does not yet know the right hypotheses. It helps explain how and why customers behave. Then use quantitative research to test how common an insight is, compare segments, or estimate demand.

Do not generalize from a small focus group as though it proves the size of a market.

### Focus groups

A focus group is a moderated discussion among potential customers that uses participant interaction to develop insights about a product or problem.

**Preparation**

- Recruit five to eight people from the potential target market, not a group of friends.
- Invite extra people in case some do not attend.
- Plan about 45–60 minutes and reserve enough setup time.
- Assign a skilled moderator and at least one additional data collector.
- Use no more than six to eight short, open, unambiguous questions.
- Move from easy and general to harder and specific; move from less personal to more personal.
- Write a guide covering purpose, participants, introductions, ground rules, questions, probes, and closing.

**During the group**

- Begin with an engagement question.
- Use exploration questions to discuss current behavior, alternatives, product, price, place, and promotion.
- Encourage participants to respond to and build on one another: the “ping-pong” effect.
- Probe neutrally: “Can you say more?” “What do you mean?” “Can you give an example?”
- Prevent domination and invite quieter voices without signaling a preferred answer.
- End with an exit question that catches missing ideas.

**Strengths:** interaction creates spontaneous ideas, one comment stimulates another, and early findings improve later surveys.

**Limits:** skilled moderation is difficult, one person can dominate, the moderator or founder can bias interpretation, and the small sample is exploratory rather than conclusive.

When analyzing qualitative data, confirm that participants represent the customer, move from description to an explanation of why behavior occurs, search for disconfirming evidence, iterate, and form hypotheses for quantitative testing.

### The 4Ps and revenue drivers

The 4Ps are:

- **Product:** offer, features, benefits, and differentiation.
- **Price:** what will be charged and why.
- **Place:** where and through which channels it will be sold.
- **Promotion:** how the target market will become aware of it.

Key revenue drivers are the number and quality of customers, purchase frequency, the selling process or channel, and price. Revenue is not established merely because a broad market spends money.

### Responsible business

Feasibility and viability should include social and environmental consequences. Responsible venture choices include honest marketing, inclusive team culture, supplier labor and environmental practices, packaging, production, end-of-life disposal, key stakeholder needs, and avoiding greenwashing or exaggerated impact claims. A “good business” creates economic value while considering how it affects people and the planet.

## 4. Full answers to the study guide's sample questions

### Sample 1: Take Kayden through an effective brainstorming session and explain why it matters

Kayden should begin by temporarily setting aside the AI shopping-platform solution and writing a customer-centered prompt, such as, “How might we help knitters move from wanting a new project to starting one with confidence?” This keeps the session focused on a researched problem instead of his preferred technology. He should invite people with different perspectives, including actual crafters, and run a brief creative warm-up. Each participant should first generate ideas silently on separate notes, which prevents Kayden or another fast speaker from anchoring the group and gives quieter members a real chance to contribute.

During group ideation, the facilitator should enforce the class rules: pursue quantity, defer judgment, encourage wild ideas, stay on topic, be visual, have one conversation at a time, and build on or combine other people's suggestions. AI can generate additional strange or varied ideas, but it should not decide which idea customers will value. After producing a large set, the team should cluster related options and sort them into grounded, blue-sky, and “spaced out” ideas. Only then should it evaluate promising options through desirability, feasibility, viability, and low-cost tests.

Brainstorming matters because Kayden has fixated on one feature-heavy answer before understanding the problem. Separating generation from evaluation helps the team avoid premature consensus, use informational diversity, and discover alternatives such as curated starter kits, project-matching help, local group coordination, or a simpler compatibility tool. The purpose is not to select the most exciting idea in the room. It is to create better hypotheses that can be tested with customers.

### Sample 2: How can Kayden establish whether Knittr is desirable, feasible, and viable?

Kayden should test **desirability first**. He needs to observe real knitters and crocheters in stores, groups, classes, and online communities and interview people outside his family. His questions should focus on the last time they chose a project, bought supplies, became stuck, changed plans, or abandoned a project. From repeated evidence, he can define a narrow target market and a customer-centered problem. He should then place a low-fidelity prototype in front of target users and watch whether they understand it, return to it, share useful feedback, and show willingness to switch or pay. This would test whether project selection and supply matching are meaningful pains and whether AI is relevant to customers at all.

For **feasibility**, Kayden should inventory what he knows, who he knows, and what resources he controls. The case shows gaps in craft expertise, programming, capital, fulfillment, and supplier relationships. He can limit risk by partnering with an experienced crafter, manually creating a small number of recommendations, using a no-code page, and fulfilling a few test orders without buying a warehouse or large inventory. He should test recommendation accuracy, user trust, access to product data, supplier reliability, shipping, privacy, returns, and the time required to deliver each order. These actions reveal whether the team can consistently provide the promised benefit.

For **viability**, Kayden needs a coherent business model. He should identify who pays, for what, how often, and through which channel. He should compare alternatives such as a product margin, subscription, affiliate fee, marketplace commission, or paid project kit. Small price tests and preorders can show willingness to pay before major investment. He must estimate customer-acquisition cost, order frequency, average revenue, product and shipping cost, returns, technology expense, inventory risk, and contribution margin. He should also map competitors and indirect substitutes, including yarn stores, Etsy, Ravelry, blogs, social media, and existing recommendation tools. Knittr becomes an opportunity only if the same focused offer is wanted by customers, deliverable by the team, and capable of producing sustainable revenue after costs.

### Sample 3: Six improved focus-group questions

Use these with five to eight actual members of a researched target segment after a neutral introduction. If a prototype is shown, wait until participants have first discussed current behavior so the product does not anchor every response.

1. **Engagement:** “Please introduce yourself by describing the last knitting or crochet project you worked on and how you chose it.”
2. **Exploration:** “Walk us through everything you did from deciding on that project to having all the supplies ready.”
3. **Exploration:** “Where did that process become confusing, frustrating, expensive, or time-consuming? Please give a specific example.”
4. **Exploration:** “How do you currently discover patterns and decide whether the pattern, yarn, cost, and skill level are a good match?”
5. **Concept exploration:** “After trying this project-and-supply-list prototype, what felt useful, what felt unhelpful or uncomfortable, and what would you change?”
6. **Exit:** “What have we not asked that we would need to understand before making this useful enough for you to choose over your current method?”

These improve Kayden's approach because they are open-ended, neutral, based on past behavior, and designed to produce stories rather than compliments. They do not assume that participants want AI, cheaper supplies, another social network, or Knittr itself. They move from an easy engagement question to detailed exploration and finish with an exit question. A neutral moderator should invite participants to respond to one another, ask for examples, manage dominant voices, and avoid defending the concept. This produces exploratory evidence instead of family approval.

## 5. Organizational behavior and team leadership

### Culture, rules, and norms

Culture is a shared system of norms, values, beliefs, and assumptions developed over time. It guides how members think and act and informally determines what is appreciated, tolerated, or punished. A formal rule says what should happen; culture is revealed by what repeatedly happens and how people respond.

Team norms are explicit agreements about behavior and interaction. Every team develops norms, even when nobody discusses them. Making them specific and shared prevents members from silently applying different expectations.

Strong norms:

- Complete the phrase “We will…”
- Describe observable behavior rather than vague values.
- Stay limited to roughly six to eight important commitments.
- Cover communication, meetings, decisions, conflict, participation, accountability, inclusion, and reflection.
- Evolve as the team encounters new conditions.

Weak norm: “We will respect each other.”

Strong norm: “We will circulate an agenda before meetings, arrive prepared, and ask someone who has not spoken before repeating our own point.”

A team charter makes implicit expectations explicit through three parts: shared aspirations, team norms, and work processes. It should be treated as a living document and revised after experience.

### Leadership and emotional intelligence

Great leadership is not explained by technical skill or position alone. Outstanding leaders tend to create strong relationships and understand how emotions affect performance.

**Resonant leaders** notice the emotional tone of a team and build connection or synchrony with others. They empower and support people rather than simply demanding compliance. Low attention to emotional tone and low synchrony produce dissonant leadership.

Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to recognize and understand emotions in oneself and others and use that awareness to manage behavior and relationships.

| EI dimension | Meaning | Useful action in a vignette |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-awareness | Recognize your emotions, triggers, strengths, weaknesses, values, and effect on others | Name that you are frustrated and notice that your tone is shutting people down |
| Self-management | Regulate impulses and manage behavior during conflict, uncertainty, or stress | Pause before responding; choose a direct but calm response instead of an angry message |
| Social awareness or empathy | Read the room and understand another person's perspective and feelings | Ask what is making a teammate hesitant and listen without immediately fixing |
| Relationship management | Shape emotional tone, coach, inspire, manage conflict and change, and foster teamwork | Clarify the issue, acknowledge feelings, facilitate a solution, and rebuild cooperation |

Self-regulation does not mean hiding all emotion. It means expressing emotion in a way that helps the situation. EI can be developed through reflection, feedback, stress awareness, deliberate pauses, curiosity, and considering others' feelings before acting.

### What makes a team effective?

Judge team effectiveness using three outcomes:

1. **Performance:** The team delivers work that meets stakeholder expectations for quality, quantity, and timeliness.
2. **Learning and adaptability:** The team improves how it works, corrects errors, manages conflict, and becomes more capable.
3. **Member well-being and growth:** Individuals learn, remain engaged, and benefit rather than being depleted or alienated.

A team that meets a deadline by exhausting members and destroying trust is not fully effective. A team that feels friendly but misses the stakeholder's requirements is not effective either.

Four broad conditions support effectiveness:

- **Group composition:** relevant skills, useful diversity, shared goals and values, social sensitivity, and no unnecessary members.
- **Relational climate:** psychological safety, mutual respect, trust, inclusion of quieter voices, support, and constructive conflict.
- **Organized process:** preparation, facilitation, timekeeping, roles, decision methods, next steps, steady pace, and explicit norms.
- **Alignment with the larger system:** clear mutual expectations, sufficient authority and resources, stakeholder feedback, and shared goals with connected teams.

### Trust

The course model is:

> Trust = Authenticity + Logic + Empathy

- **Authenticity:** I believe you are genuine and consistent.
- **Logic:** I believe your reasoning and competence.
- **Empathy:** I believe you understand and care about my experience or interests.

In a vignette, diagnose which part is weak. A capable teammate who hides information may have a problem with authenticity. A caring teammate who makes unsupported claims may have a logic problem. A technically correct leader who humiliates others has an empathy problem.

### Psychological safety

Psychological safety is a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Members can ask questions, admit uncertainty, raise concerns, disagree, offer unusual ideas, and discuss errors without expecting intimidation, shame, exclusion, disrespect, or rejection.

It is a team climate, not merely private trust between two people. It does not mean low standards, constant agreement, or freedom from accountability. A psychologically safe team can debate strongly while protecting people's ability to speak.

Actions that build it include:

- Leaders acknowledge their own fallibility.
- Ask directly for concerns and dissent, especially from lower-status or quiet members.
- Respond to bad news and unusual ideas with curiosity before evaluation.
- Distribute conversational turns and prevent domination.
- Thank people for surfacing errors.
- Separate the person from the idea or behavior being discussed.
- Follow up on concerns so speaking up has visible value.

Psychological safety supports learning, innovation, performance, and productive failure. Strong teams may report more errors because members feel safe enough to reveal and correct them, not because they necessarily commit more errors.

### Failure versus mistake

- **Failure** is an unwanted result from trying something genuinely new, uncertain, or unpredictable. It creates information and should be examined and learned from.
- **Mistake** is predictable wrongdoing, such as repeating a known error, ignoring an established process, or failing to use available information. It should be prevented and corrected.

In the hidden-path exercise, testing an unexplored square and hearing a beep was a useful failure. Choosing the same known-wrong square again was a mistake. Teams should celebrate intelligent exploration without removing accountability for negligence.

### Constructive conflict

Conflict is inevitable and is not automatically harmful. Task-focused disagreement can expose assumptions and improve a decision. Relationship or status conflict attacks people, damages trust, and distracts from the goal.

Useful responses include restating the shared goal, separating interests from personalities, asking for evidence, giving each person airtime, naming emotional escalation, agreeing on a decision process, and documenting the final decision and next steps.

### Team composition, diversity, and collective intelligence

Diversity of expertise, background, network, demographics, capabilities, opinions, values, personalities, and preferences can improve creativity and reduce groupthink. It can also make communication and trust harder if the team does not create enabling norms.

**Informational diversity** brings task-relevant knowledge and productive cognitive disagreement. **Value or relationship conflict** can turn disagreement into personal or status conflict. Leaders should invite and validate dissent while maintaining emotional bonds.

**Faultlines** divide a team into stable subgroups. Strong faultlines can cause members to share information only within a faction. Reduce them by creating cross-subgroup tasks, shared goals, inclusive norms, and a broader team identity.

Collective intelligence depends less on a few stars than on social sensitivity and roughly equal conversational turn-taking. Teams need to surface what different members know.

### Process gains and process losses

Teams create **process gains** when collaboration produces more than individuals could separately. Gains include motivation, shared identity, combined knowledge, error checking, role modeling, and a shared mental map of expertise.

Teams suffer **process losses** through social loafing, coordination problems, fear of being wrong, dysfunctional conflict, failure to share unique information, information overload, dominant speakers, and premature consensus or groupthink.

A **transactive memory system** is the team's shared understanding of who knows what. Clear expertise and role maps help the team direct questions and information to the right person without expecting everyone to know everything.

### Design, launch, and process management

The Leading Teams reading identifies three leadership levers:

1. **Team design:** Set the why, who, what, when, and how. Create a clear, challenging, consequential purpose; choose the needed mix and size; define meaningful roles; set a realistic lifespan; and align rewards and resources.
2. **Team launch:** Build shared understanding of purpose, available capabilities, norms, strategy, and leadership. Teams often rush this stage and pay for it later.
3. **Process management:** Monitor progress, balance competing needs, and intervene at useful moments. If the original launch was weak, the team may need an explicit relaunch.

Design usually has the greatest effect, followed by launch. Coaching cannot fully compensate for a team that lacks the ability, authority, resources, or clarity required by its task.

Team purpose should be:

- **Clear:** people know where they are going and how success will be measured.
- **Challenging:** the task creates energy and requires a team.
- **Consequential:** members understand why the result matters.

Team roles should clarify division of labor and accountability while offering skill variety, a whole identifiable contribution, significance, autonomy, and direct feedback when possible.

### Team development and useful interventions

The forming–storming–norming–performing pattern is a useful lens, not a guaranteed law. Teams also often change their behavior near the midpoint of a deadline, shifting from planning toward execution.

Useful process management includes:

- Making the team's current stage and progress visible.
- Balancing process with outcomes.
- Balancing internal teamwork with information from customers and stakeholders.
- Balancing exploration with execution.
- Using motivational intervention near launch, consultative intervention near the midpoint, and educational reflection near the end when those fit the case.

### DiSC behavioral styles

DiSC is a shared language for behavioral preferences, not a judgment of competence or a rigid label. Everyone uses all four styles and may prefer one or two. The person remains the final judge of whether a profile fits.

| Style | Typical strengths | Potential challenge | Best way to communicate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D — Dominance/Driver | Direct, fast, decisive, results-focused, willing to take risks | Impatient, blunt, may overlook input | Be brief and direct; lead with the result, options, and next step |
| I — Influencing/Expressive | Energetic, persuasive, social, enthusiastic, generates big ideas | May skip detail or act before thinking | Invite ideas and connection; summarize important details and deadlines in writing |
| S — Steady/Supporter | Calm, dependable, patient, cooperative, trust-building | May avoid hard conversations or need time with change | Be calm and supportive; explain changes and allow time to respond |
| C — Conscientious/Compliant/Analytical | Accurate, logical, careful, structured, quality-focused | May overanalyze or become stuck in details | Be organized and accurate; bring evidence, expectations, and sufficient thinking time |

The goal is to **flex**, not demand that others adapt to you. A D can pause and invite input. An I can document commitments. An S can raise disagreement earlier. A C can identify the minimum evidence needed for a timely decision.

When teams change membership, veteran members should step back enough to create shared ownership, while new members should step in and contribute. The whole team must rebuild trust, psychological safety, goals, and norms.

## 6. OB practice mini-cases with model answers

These are predicted practice questions based on the stated exam format, not leaked exam questions.

### Practice 1: A quiet teammate stops sharing after being mocked

**Prompt:** During ideation, Marcus laughs at Ana's unusual product idea. Ana says nothing for the rest of the meeting, and the team quickly approves Marcus's original proposal. Diagnose the problem and recommend a response.

**Model answer:** The team has weak psychological safety, a shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe. Marcus's laugh turns disagreement into a social penalty, and Ana's silence is evidence that speaking now feels risky. The quick vote also suggests poor airtime allocation and premature consensus, so the team may lose Ana's unique information. The facilitator should stop, acknowledge the dismissive response, ask Ana what led to the idea, and invite questions before evaluation. The team should add a norm that each member contributes during divergence and that criticism waits until the evaluation stage. This would make dissent safer and improve learning and creativity.

### Practice 2: The leader sends an angry message after a missed deadline

**Prompt:** A team leader sees an incomplete draft, assumes the assigned member does not care, and posts an angry accusation in the group chat. The member explains later that the role and due date were never confirmed.

**Model answer:** The leader shows weak self-awareness and self-management, two dimensions of emotional intelligence. Frustration may be reasonable, but the leader acts on it before checking the facts, and the public accusation can damage trust and psychological safety. The vague role and deadline also reveal an organized-process failure rather than only an individual motivation problem. The leader should pause, ask what each person understood, acknowledge the premature accusation, and clarify the owner, deliverable, standard, and deadline in writing. The team should end future meetings with documented next steps. This addresses both the emotional damage and the process that caused the confusion.

### Practice 3: A high-D and high-C teammate cannot agree

**Prompt:** Devon wants to decide immediately and begin selling. Casey keeps asking for more data and refuses to approve the plan. Each thinks the other is hurting the team.

**Model answer:** Their conflict reflects different DiSC preferences. Devon's D style values speed and results, while Casey's C style values evidence, accuracy, and quality. Neither style is inherently better; the problem is that both treat a preference as the only correct process. Casey should give Devon a brief summary of the highest-risk evidence, and Devon should state the decision and deadline clearly while allowing a defined review period. Together they can agree on the minimum evidence required for a reversible small test. This lets Devon preserve momentum and Casey protect the team from avoidable errors.

### Practice 4: The team meets its deadline, but members are exhausted and distrustful

**Prompt:** A team earns a high grade, but two members did nearly all the work, nobody learned a new role, and several members refuse to work together again. Was the team effective?

**Model answer:** The team achieved only one of the three effectiveness outcomes. Its performance met the stakeholder's immediate standard, but the process did not build learning or adaptability, and member well-being declined. Unequal work also suggests social loafing, poor role design, or weak accountability. The team should redistribute identifiable work, document owners and checkpoints, and use a midpoint review to correct imbalances before the deadline. It should also discuss what each person wants to learn. These changes protect output while making the team more capable and sustainable.

### Practice 5: A failed experiment is punished

**Prompt:** Priya tests a new sales script after receiving team approval. It performs worse than the old version. The team tells her that failure is unacceptable and decides never to experiment again.

**Model answer:** The team is confusing failure with a mistake. Priya tried an approved method under uncertainty, so the disappointing result is an intelligent failure that supplies evidence. Punishing it will reduce psychological safety and cause members to hide results or avoid future learning. The team should compare the scripts, record what the test revealed, and design the next limited experiment. Accountability would be appropriate if Priya ignored a known procedure or repeated the same disproven action without a reason, but that is not what happened here.

### Practice 6: Two subgroups stop sharing information

**Prompt:** The original pitch members make product decisions privately, while new members discuss marketing among themselves. Important information does not cross between the groups.

**Model answer:** The team has formed a strong faultline between veterans and newcomers. That division weakens shared ownership and creates a process loss because information stays inside subgroups. The team should relaunch: restate the shared purpose, map everyone's expertise, assign cross-subgroup work, agree on decision rights, and create a norm that consequential decisions occur in the full team or are documented for it. Veterans should step back from automatic control, and new members should step in early. These actions build a wider team identity and a useful transactive memory system.

### Practice 7: A caring leader cannot persuade the team

**Prompt:** Jordan checks on everyone and is clearly sincere, but proposes major spending without evidence or a clear explanation. Team members still do not trust the decision.

**Model answer:** The trust model shows that authenticity and empathy are present, but logic is weak. Caring and sincerity do not replace sound reasoning. Jordan should explain the decision criteria, show evidence and alternatives, name uncertainty, and propose a limited test before a full commitment. That would strengthen the logic side of trust while preserving the relational strengths already present.

### Practice 8: An informal culture rewards lateness

**Prompt:** The team's written rule says meetings begin at 6:00 p.m., but meetings normally wait for two influential members who arrive at 6:15. People who arrive on time begin coming late too.

**Model answer:** The formal rule says 6:00, but the culture rewards lateness because the team's repeated behavior shows that influential members face no consequence. Culture is the shared system that informally signals what is accepted, so the lived norm has overpowered the stated rule. The team should begin at 6:00, document decisions for absent members, and apply the same expectation to everyone. Consistent reinforcement would turn punctuality from a written wish into an actual norm.

## 7. Rapid-recall sheet

### Entrepreneurship

- ET&A = choose between prediction and CreAction; use small, affordable experiments under uncertainty.
- Idea ≠ opportunity. Opportunity = specific customer + meaningful problem + value + workable economics.
- Start with desirability, then test feasibility and viability; eventually all three must intersect.
- Empathize through observation and open interviews; define from the customer's perspective.
- Brainstorm: quantity, focus, visual, build, one conversation, defer judgment, wild ideas.
- AI generates options; humans and customers evaluate them; verify AI evidence.
- Customer pays; consumer uses; identify both.
- Target segment is narrow and chosen; persona must come from real data.
- Value proposition = resonate + differentiate + substantiate.
- Feature tells what it is; benefit tells why the customer cares.
- Business model = create, deliver, and capture value through nine connected canvas blocks.
- Qualitative explores how/why; quantitative confirms how many/how often/how much.
- Focus group = 5–8 target participants, skilled neutral moderator, 6–8 open questions, interaction, exploratory findings.
- 4Ps = product, price, place, promotion.
- Revenue drivers = customers, frequency, selling process/channel, price.

### Organizational behavior

- Culture = shared norms, values, beliefs, and assumptions reinforced over time.
- Team charter = aspirations + norms + work processes.
- EI = self-awareness + self-management + social awareness + relationship management.
- Resonant leader = reads emotional tone and builds synchrony.
- Team effectiveness = performance + learning/adaptability + member well-being/growth.
- Team conditions = composition + relational climate + organized process + larger-system alignment.
- Trust = authenticity + logic + empathy.
- Psychological safety = safe interpersonal risk-taking; it is compatible with high standards.
- Failure explores uncertainty; mistake violates known information or repeats a known error.
- Productive conflict debates tasks and evidence; destructive conflict attacks people or status.
- Process gains add motivation/knowledge; process losses include loafing, domination, coordination problems, hidden information, and groupthink.
- Team leadership levers = design + launch + process management.
- DiSC: D wants results, I wants ideas/connection, S wants stability/cooperation, C wants accuracy/logic.
- Flex style to the other person; do not use DiSC as an excuse or label.
